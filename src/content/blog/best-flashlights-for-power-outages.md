**Quick answer:** For a blackout you want **runtime, not lumens**: a light that does 50–300 lumens for days beats a 3,000-lumen light that dies in an hour. The kit that works has three layers. A **lantern** lights the room (the [Coleman LED lantern](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HF226B4/?tag=lightertorch-20) on 4 D cells, or the USB-C [Fenix CL28R](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BSB4BNNG/?tag=lightertorch-20), which can also charge a phone). A **headlamp** per person keeps your hands free (the AAA-powered [Black Diamond Spot 400](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H1R7P6L2/?tag=lightertorch-20)). One strong **handheld** handles the breaker box, the yard and the storm damage (the [Fenix PD36R V2.0](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H73HZH2Y/?tag=lightertorch-20), up to 482 hours on its lowest mode).

Outages aren't only a hurricane-season problem. Ready.gov notes that a winter storm can [last a few hours or several days and cut off heat, power and communication](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather), and its outage advice starts with [having flashlights for every household member](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages). Build the kit before the forecast turns, because emergency lights sell out fast once a storm is on the map.

## Why Your Phone Flashlight Isn't a Plan

The phone light is fine for finding the real flashlight — and terrible for everything else. It drains the one battery you need most for emergency calls and outage updates, it can't be set down to light a room, and multi-day outages after a big storm turn "my phone is my flashlight" into "my phone is dead." Rule one of outage lighting: **never spend phone battery on light.**

## The Three-Layer Blackout Kit

### Layer 1: A lantern for the room

A lantern lights a whole kitchen or bedroom at once and sits on the table while everyone eats, plays cards, or waits it out. Look for: 360° output with a low mode in the 50–100 lumen range (that's genuinely enough), **100+ hours of low-mode runtime**, and AA/D-cell or USB-C power. Our two picks cover both camps. The [Coleman LED lantern](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HF226B4/?tag=lightertorch-20) runs on 4 D batteries (sold separately) for up to 14 hours on high, and D cells sit in a drawer for years. The [Fenix CL28R](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BSB4BNNG/?tag=lightertorch-20) is the rechargeable one: up to 2,000 lumens, more than 15 days on its low setting, and USB-C and USB-A outputs so it doubles as a small power bank for a phone. More rechargeable options, including a hand-crank model, are in our [rechargeable lantern guide](/best-rechargeable-camping-lanterns/).

### Layer 2: A headlamp per person

The most underrated outage tool. Cooking on a camp stove, taping windows, checking the sump pump, walking the dog — everything during an outage needs two hands. A basic 200–400 lumen headlamp with a red mode (preserves night vision, doesn't wake the household) costs little and changes everything. One per family member; they're no fun to share. Models with a AAA fallback are the smart pick here, for the same reason they win on long trips — see our [best headlamps guide](/best-headlamps-for-camping-and-running/). Our pick is the [Black Diamond Spot 400](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H1R7P6L2/?tag=lightertorch-20): 400 lumens, a red night-vision mode and three included AAA batteries, with Black Diamond's optional BD 1500 rechargeable pack if you want one. AAA cells are the easiest batteries to find in a store during an outage.

### Layer 3: One serious handheld

For everything outside the house: downed branches, the breaker panel, signaling, walking a dark street. This is where our usual advice applies — see [things to look for in a good LED flashlight](/how-many-lumens-should-flashlight-have-2/#what-else-to-look-for-besides-lumens) — but for storms, weight **waterproofing (IPX7+)** and a **low mode with 100+ hour runtime** over maximum lumens. Our pick is the same light we recommend everywhere: the [Fenix PD36R V2.0](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H73HZH2Y/?tag=lightertorch-20): 1,700 lumens when you need power, and [up to 482 hours on its 5-lumen Eco mode or 50 hours on 50-lumen Low](https://help.fenix-store.com/en-US/fenix-pd36r-v20-reference-manual-and-specifications-8113488) when you need endurance, IP68, USB-C. One outage detail from Fenix's manual: only Eco works while the light is charging, so charge it before the storm, not during. If you'd rather not depend on charging at all, the [Streamlight PolyTac](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B001S0IN46/?tag=lightertorch-20) runs on two included lithium cells, is IPX7 waterproof and puts out 600 lumens.

## The Battery Strategy (This Is the Part People Get Wrong)

| Approach | Strength | Weakness | Role in the kit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rechargeable (USB-C) | Cheap to run, always topped up | Dead is dead when the grid's down | Primary lights |
| AA / D alkaline | Shelf-stable ~10 years, buy anywhere | Weaker output, single-use | Backup lights + lantern |
| Power bank | Recharges lights AND phones | Needs discipline to keep charged | The bridge between them |

The resilient setup mixes chemistries: rechargeable primaries, alkaline-powered backups, and a [high-capacity power bank](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=power+bank+20000mah&tag=lightertorch-20) (or two) kept charged. Fenix says to [recharge a stored PD36R every four months](https://www.fenixlighting.com/products/fenix-pd36r-rechargeable-flashlight), and that's a good rule for every rechargeable in the kit. When a storm enters the forecast, top up every rechargeable device that night — not when the wind starts. Spare alkalines live *in* the kit, not in the kitchen drawer where they wander off. For why we prefer USB-C charging across the board, see the [USB-C flashlight guide](/best-usb-c-rechargeable-flashlights/).

## Fire, Candles, and the Old Ways

![A long-neck lighter that has just lit a pillar candle in a dark kitchen during a power outage, rain on the window behind.](/uploads/2026/09/blackout-kitchen-candle-lit-by-long-lighter.webp)

Candles are a real fire risk in an outage: the National Candle Association, citing NFPA data, says [more than 20% of candle fires involving fatalities happen during a loss of power](https://candles.org/fire-safety-tips/), and the Red Cross's advice is simply to [use flashlights, not candles](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/power-outage.html). If you use them anyway, never leave one burning unattended, and put them in real holders away from curtains. The safer modern equivalent for ambiance and ignition both: an [arc lighter](/best-arc-lighters-for-camping/) for the gas stove (most gas ranges light manually in an outage — here's [how to light a gas stove manually](/manually-lighting-a-gas-stove/)) and LED lanterns for the light. One rule matters more than any light: Ready.gov warns [never to heat your home with a gas stove or oven](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages), and camp stoves, grills and generators belong outdoors, at least 20 feet from windows, because of carbon monoxide. Put a battery-backed CO detector on every level. If you do stock a lighter in the kit, a fresh BIC keeps for years, as we covered in [Zippo vs BIC vs arc](/zippo-vs-bic-vs-arc-lighter/).

## The 10-Minute Storm-Kit Checklist

- [ ] 1 lantern per main room (with its batteries stored beside it, not inside it)
- [ ] 1 headlamp per person + red mode
- [ ] 1 waterproof handheld (charged monthly)
- [ ] Spare alkalines: at least 8×AA / 4×D in the kit box
- [ ] 1–2 power banks, topped up on the first of every month
- [ ] Arc lighter or fresh BIC for the stove
- [ ] A flashlight in the car, too ([Ready.gov's winter car kit](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather) includes one)
- [ ] Battery-backed carbon monoxide detector on every level
- [ ] Everything in ONE labeled box the whole family can find in the dark

## FAQ

**How many lumens do I need in a power outage?** Less than you think: 50–100 for a room, 200–400 for moving around, 1,000+ only for outdoor checks. Runtime is the spec that matters — our [lumens guide](/how-many-lumens-should-flashlight-have-2/) has the full breakdown.

**Are solar lights worth it?** As a backup layer, yes — a small solar lantern recharges on any windowsill. As the primary, no; storm weeks are cloudy weeks.

**Should I buy a generator instead?** Different problem. A generator runs the fridge; you still need portable light in every room and pocket. Build the light kit first — it costs a tenth as much.

**What's the best flashlight for a winter storm power outage?** One with a long low mode and batteries you can keep charged or stocked. Our pick is the [Fenix PD36R V2.0](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H73HZH2Y/?tag=lightertorch-20), with up to 482 hours on Eco. Add a lantern for the room and a headlamp per person, because a winter outage can last several days.

**How many flashlights do I need for a power outage?** At least one per person, which is [Ready.gov's advice](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages), plus a lantern for each room you'll actually use and one light in the car.

**Can I use a gas stove for heat during a power outage?** No. Ready.gov says never to heat your home with a gas stovetop or oven, because of carbon monoxide and fire risk. Cooking on the burners, lit by hand, is a different thing; see [how to light a gas stove manually](/manually-lighting-a-gas-stove/).

**How often should I check the kit?** Test lights and rotate batteries twice a year — the classic trick is doing it when the clocks change.
